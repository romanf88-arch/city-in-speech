# The City in Speech

A walk through Plato's Republic, set at the harbor of Athens on the day after the conversation. You hear what was said the night before, listen to people who disagree about what it means, and decide what you think.

You have also come to the harbor to collect a debt for your family. The people who can help you find the debtor tell you what they know only after you have done something for them. When you find him, you decide what to do about the debt.

Play it here: https://romanf88-arch.github.io/city-in-speech/

- It runs in a desktop browser with a keyboard and mouse. Chrome or Edge is recommended. It needs a keyboard, so it is not for phones.
- You need no background in philosophy. A full run takes about two hours.
- Progress is saved in your own browser. Nothing is sent anywhere.

## The single prompt version 1

The first version of the game is kept as it was on 11 November 2026, before any changes made after playing it: https://romanf88-arch.github.io/city-in-speech/v1/

It covers the same five parts of the Republic, but it has no guide, no errand, and no houses, and its people and sky are the older low-polygon ones. Its saved game is kept apart from the current version. The files are in `v1/`.

## Controls

W and S walk, A and D turn, drag the mouse to look around, Shift runs, E talks, H asks your guide for help, J opens the journal, M opens the map, Esc pauses.

## Sources and credits

- What Socrates and the others argue is from Plato's Republic. The retellings are the game's own. A few short phrases on the passage cards are from C. D. C. Reeve's translation (Hackett, 2004).
- The guide, her purse, and the six readers in the game are invented. Each reader speaks for real books, which the journal cites by page: Julia Annas, Allan Bloom, Seth Benardete, Eric Havelock, Karl Popper, C. D. C. Reeve, Sean McAleer (CC BY 4.0), and essays in The Cambridge Companion to Plato's Republic and Plato's Republic: A Critical Guide.
- You can walk into three houses: a schoolroom, the shield workshop of the family of Cephalus, and a ship captain's house. What they show of daily life is from ancient texts, read in older translations from the Perseus Digital Library: Plato's Protagoras, Lysis, Apology and Phaedrus, Xenophon, Lysias, Aeschines and Aristophanes. The schoolroom follows a cup painted by Douris (Berlin, Antikensammlung F 2285), and the loom follows an oil flask by the Amasis Painter (New York, Metropolitan Museum 31.11.10). The journal names the passage for each claim and lists what is invented.
- Your errand is invented: the debt, the debtor, the man he owes and that man's doorkeeper, and the tasks people set you. What the tasks show of Athenian life is from ancient texts, read in older translations from the Perseus Digital Library: Aristophanes' Peace, Wasps and Acharnians, Lysias, Demosthenes, Herodotus, Plato's Apology and Theaetetus, and Xenophon's On Hunting. The journal names the passage for each claim and says what is made up.
- The reciter in the market square recites the whole Iliad in Samuel Butler's translation (1898, public domain).
- The people are built from the MakeHuman base model (released as CC0), with their clothes draped by cloth simulation in Blender.
- Built with three.js (MIT licence; see `vendor/THREE-LICENSE.txt`).
